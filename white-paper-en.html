Executive summary

Learning continuity after class

Schools teach in groups, but much of practice, review, and consolidation happens individually after class. Homework and other forms of at-home review have long helped serve that function. The challenge is that the conditions required to continue learning at home are not equally available to every child.

International organizations use the concept of the last mile across several dimensions of education: the OECD discusses connectivity reaching students' homes and personal devices; UNICEF refers to last-mile learners; and the World Bank uses the term last-mile families when addressing access to learning materials at home.

Source: OECD 2023 ↗  ·  Source: UNICEF 2022 ↗  ·  Source: World Bank — Read@Home ↗

School teaches. SVEED supports the review.

SVEED addresses one concrete expression of that last mile: the gap between content taught in the classroom and the individual review and consolidation that happens afterward. Guided curriculum review is the mechanism; continuity of learning is the function.

1 · The last mile

The last mile extends beyond the school building

“Last mile” is not a term invented by SVEED. The OECD devotes a section of its Digital Education Outlook 2023 to the last mile problem in digital education and notes that this last mile can extend from schools into students' homes and personal devices. Source: OECD 2023 ↗

UNICEF uses the term last-mile learners for children who are being left behind and are among the hardest to reach. UNESCO also uses last-mile language when discussing the challenge of reaching the most disadvantaged and hardest-to-reach learners. Source: UNICEF 2022 ↗   Source: UNESCO — EFA Global Monitoring Report ↗

At-home review has long served part of this function

The need for continuity after class is not new. Homework is a long-standing instructional practice that can extend classroom concepts, support self-regulation, and contribute to learning. OECD evidence also points to the importance of task design and indicates that short, regular assignments can provide benefits without unnecessarily widening inequities. Source: PISA 2022 ↗

The challenge is that the conditions needed to complete that review are not equally distributed. PISA 2018 links lower homework participation among disadvantaged students to factors such as lack of a quiet place to study, unequal access to internet and computers, and less available parental support. In its Brazil-specific analysis, the OECD also documents substantial inequalities in access to learning resources at home. Source: PISA 2018 ↗   Source: OECD — Education in Brazil ↗

The World Bank addresses a related dimension through Read@Home, an initiative designed to bring reading and learning materials into households and reach vulnerable families with limited access to those resources. Source: World Bank — Read@Home ↗

SVEED does not begin from the assumption that schools, teachers, or families have “failed.” It begins from a structural reality: learning continuity after class depends on conditions that vary widely across students. Its purpose is to reduce some of those dependencies through a simple, guided review system.

2 · Scope

SVEED reviews; it does not replace school, teachers, or the curriculum

SVEED is a lightweight Android-based system for continuity of learning. Its central mechanism is guided individual curriculum review: revisiting, practicing, offering hints, recovering prerequisites when needed, and maintaining a short study routine around skills the school has already taught.

SVEED does not claim to solve education's entire last-mile problem on its own. It does not provide internet access, distribute devices, or replace public policy. It was designed to reduce concrete barriers: dependence on continuous connectivity, constant adult mediation, and the break between classroom instruction and individual review at home.

What SVEED does

  • reviews curriculum skills that have already been taught;
  • generates exercises locally from rules and templates;
  • offers progressive hints when available;
  • provides supporting concepts separately from hints;
  • temporarily reduces difficulty when friction appears;
  • returns to the target skill after stabilization.

Deliberate limits

  • does not replace a teacher or classroom instruction;
  • should not autonomously introduce new curriculum content;
  • does not semantically diagnose the cause of each error;
  • does not use points, coins, rankings, or streaks as the engine of the session;
  • does not depend on generative AI to create questions for the child.

3 · Pedagogical flow

Error, hint, retry, and continuity

Target skill

The system starts from a skill previously identified through the student flow or teacher mode.

Analogous exercise

The child practices an instance generated locally within a closed curriculum universe.

First error

When a hint is available, SVEED offers support without immediately turning the error into visual punishment.

Second chance

The child can use the hint and try again. If another error occurs, the system provides the feedback needed to continue.

Hints may exist in up to three levels, depending on the available content. The Concept Library is a different layer: while a hint guides reasoning within that exercise, the library explains the related concept.

Error is not treated merely as a score. It acts as a signal to adjust the experience, provide support, and preserve continuity in the review.

4 · Rule-based adaptation

Orbit, cognitive breathing room, and prerequisite recovery

Review works around a target skill with limited local variation. The orbit consists of TARGET, TARGET-1, TARGET-2, and TARGET+1.

TARGETThe main skill the child needs to review.
TARGET-1A temporary reduction in complexity to maintain flow and reduce cognitive load.
TARGET-2A second reduction step when difficulty persists.
TARGET+1A controlled probe above the target. If difficulty appears, the system quickly returns.

Moving down does not mean abandoning the objective. It can work as cognitive breathing room: the child returns to a relatively simpler range, interrupts a frustrating sequence of errors, and then returns to the target.

When difficulty persists, the curriculum graph can be used to step back to an associated prerequisite. The graph is used only downward; it is not a mechanism for pushing the child into new curriculum progressions.

Operational formulation: when successive errors indicate difficulty moving forward, SVEED can step back, through predefined rules, to an associated curriculum prerequisite and then guide the student back to the original path.

5 · Child experience

Simple on the surface, complex only where necessary

The child does not need to understand the orbit, graph, templates, or recovery rules. The experience presents a predictable sequence, few controls, and support when needed.

TTS as an autonomy layer

The operating system's text-to-speech can read prompts and instructions. For children with lower reading fluency, this reduces dependence on an adult reader. Older, fluent students can simply ignore the audio.

No gamification and no scoreboard during review

SVEED does not use coins, rankings, avatars, or artificial rewards as the core of engagement. It also avoids continuously displaying a score of correct and incorrect answers during the session. The feedback shown is what is necessary to conduct the review.

6 · Routine and focus

A short, predictable session with a safe exit

The core product is a short daily review designed around approximately 15 minutes. The intention is to create a routine that can be repeated, not a second school day.

During review, SVEED can activate a focus mode that reduces impulsive switching to competing apps. This is not an absolute lock: incoming calls remain available, and an emergency exit allows the session to end when necessary.

The routine also includes a limited number of invitations throughout the day and a closing time, avoiding pressure on the child to complete the activity at any hour of the night.

7 · Engineering

Local content, explicit rules, and low infrastructure dependence

The application is designed to operate locally on Android. Curriculum content, prerequisite graphs, templates, and rules are embedded on the device.

Offline-first

The educational session does not depend on continuous connectivity.

Local generation

Questions are produced from rules and templates, with controlled variation and no calls to generative models.

Explicit pedagogical logic

Orbit, return to target, hints, and prerequisite recovery follow rules defined in the product.

Low technical marginal cost

The architecture reduces network use, backend dependence, and compute cost per session.

The pedagogical logic is rule-based and auditable, but variation generation uses local randomness. For this reason, this version does not describe the system as exact reproduction or full determinism for each exercise.

8 · Data and security

Data minimization, with limits described explicitly

SVEED does not require the child's name, national ID number, email, or phone number to run the review. The architecture does, however, use persistent technical identifiers and session records. The accurate description is therefore minimization and pseudonymization, not absolute anonymization.

Current records can track elements such as time, subject, skill, correct answers, errors, and hint use at the session level. They do not currently reconstruct the full event chain of every individual exercise.

The product should not be described as “tamper-proof,” “absolutely anonymous,” or as providing “guaranteed cryptographic integrity” until the corresponding mechanisms are effectively connected and validated in the production flow.

9 · School integration

Teacher mode: the school defines the target; SVEED conducts the review

Teacher mode allows dates and curriculum skill codes to be entered to guide review at home. The objective is to preserve alignment with what is being worked on in class.

In the product's current state, the input accepts more than one skill in the plan, but daily execution still uses the first valid skill for that day. This limitation is described here to avoid making a promise beyond the current implementation.

11 · Evidence

The next maturity step is external, not merely technical

The current stage combines a functional product, technical testing, and formative observation, but it does not yet include a formal external outcomes study. The appropriate next step is an evaluation proportionate to the nature of the product.

A useful evaluation may observe, among other points, the child's autonomy over the first days of use, continuity of the routine, hint use, recovery after difficulty, and learning outcomes compatible with the review scope.

The proposed strategy is sequential: consolidate the product and its institutional presentation; obtain external guidance on evidence design; run a documented pilot; and then evaluate expansion and partnerships.

Simple for the child to use. Complex only where necessary: in the logic that keeps review appropriate, continuous, and low-friction.

12 · Scalability and governance

The GovTech layer remains relevant — but it is no longer the primary identity

The offline-first architecture, session records, and the possibility of aggregating data can be useful in deployments with schools, public education systems, operators, and institutional partners. This auditability layer remains relevant.

The strategic order, however, is different: first, a clear educational product that is externally evaluated; then, when the appropriate context and partners exist, auditability can function as an additional capability for deployment at scale.

In this version, SVEED does not claim that its architecture is a specific legal requirement imposed by oversight bodies, nor that use of the system by itself constitutes proof of compliance.
